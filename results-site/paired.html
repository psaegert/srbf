Paired comparisons

Every method gets the same problems, so two methods A and B can be compared problem by problem: on each problem \(k\), take A's value minus B's, \(\Delta_k = x_A^{(k)} - x_B^{(k)}\), and look at these differences. This is more sensitive than comparing two averages. Some problems are hard for every method, which widens the interval of each average, but that shared difficulty cancels in the difference. So two intervals can overlap even when one method is better problem after problem.

Rates

For a rate, only the problems where the two methods disagree can tell them apart: the \(b\) problems that A recovers and B does not, and the \(c\) problems that B recovers and A does not, out of \(K\) problems. The exact McNemar test gives the probability of an imbalance between \(b\) and \(c\) at least this large if both methods were equally good (the p-value), and the difference of the two rates comes with a 95 % Wald interval:

$$ p = \min\!\Big\{1,\; 2\, P\big[\operatorname{Bin}(b + c, \tfrac{1}{2}) \le \min(b, c)\big]\Big\}, \qquad \hat{\Delta} = \frac{b - c}{K} \;\pm\; \frac{z}{K} \sqrt{b + c - \frac{(b - c)^2}{K}} . $$

Continuous metrics

For the other metrics, the view takes the problems where both methods have a finite value: an exact fit counts at the floor of log10 FVU, and a formula that blows up is left out. It reports the mean difference \(\bar{\Delta}\) with its 95 % interval, and the problems where the method is better or worse than the other on the metric's own terms (for a ratio: closer to 1). An exact sign test asks whether one method is better on more problems than chance would give; it can disagree with the interval of the mean, because a few large differences move the mean. Ratios are compared on a log scale and shown as factors: \(\times\, 0.5\) means half. Each comparison pairs the two methods on the same problem in the same run, so both had exactly the same data. Every run that both methods have finished counts, so a problem usually counts twice.
