Paired comparisons

Every method gets the same problems, so two methods A and B can be compared problem by problem: on each problem \(k\), take A's value minus B's, \(\Delta_k = x_A^{(k)} - x_B^{(k)}\), and look at these differences. A problem's value is the average of its runs. The differences are averaged over the problem sets like any value (see How values are averaged), with a 95 % interval. This is more sensitive than comparing two averages. Some problems are hard for every method, which widens the interval of each average, but that shared difficulty cancels in the difference. So two intervals can overlap even when one method is better problem after problem.

Rates

For a rate, a problem's difference is A's share of hits over its runs minus B's; it is zero wherever the two methods agree, so only the problems where they disagree move it. The p-value is the probability of an average difference at least this large if both methods were equally good. It comes from a t-test over the problem sets, the same that gives the interval: with \(S\) problem sets, weights \(w_s\) and set averages \(\bar{\Delta}_s\),

$$ t = \frac{\hat{\Delta}}{\sqrt{\sum_s w_s\, (\bar{\Delta}_s - \hat{\Delta})^2 \,/\, \big((S - 1) \sum_s w_s\big)}}, \qquad p = 2\, P\big[\,T_{S-1} \ge |t|\,\big] . $$

Continuous metrics

For the other metrics, the difference \(\hat{\Delta}\) takes the problems where both methods have a finite value. Two formulas that both meet Numeric Recovery tie: here log10 FVU is read no lower than \(\log_{10} 2^{-23} \approx -6.92\), the recovery criterion, so two recovered formulas are not told apart by their error below it. (Means go further down, to -15.65, the precision of a 64-bit float.) Ratios are compared on a log scale and shown as factors: \(\times\, 0.5\) means half. The p-value does not test \(\hat{\Delta}\), which a few very large differences can move. It tests how often A does better than B on a problem: each problem's superiority is the share of run pairs A wins minus the share it loses, on the metric's own terms (for a ratio: closer to 1; a formula that is missing or blows up is worst), and the t-test above asks whether its average over the problem sets could be zero. The better / worse counts show on how many problems A does better or worse. Both methods see the same formula on a problem, with points sampled from the same ranges, though each run's points are drawn anew. So the pairing removes differences in how hard the problems are.
