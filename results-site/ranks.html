Ranks

Ranks answer another question: how do all methods compare at once? On each problem, the methods are placed 1st, 2nd, and so on by the chosen metric. A method's standing is the place it takes on average.

What a rank measures

Every problem hands out the same places, so easy and hard problems count the same, and a narrow win counts as much as a wide one. Ranks therefore measure how consistently a method is ahead, while the paired comparisons measure by how much. A method without a usable formula for a problem is placed last on it, on every metric, and two formulas that both meet Numeric Recovery tie.

From pairs to places

Places are built from pairs. For two methods \(i\) and \(j\) on one problem, take the chance that \(i\) does better than \(j\), setting every run of one against every run of the other and counting a tie as half. This chance is averaged over the problem sets like any value (see How values are averaged), giving \(P_{ij}\), with \(P_{ij} + P_{ji} = 1\). A method's average place among \(m\) methods is

$$ \bar{r}_j = 1 + \sum_{i \ne j} P_{ij} , $$

one plus the chances that each other method does better than it. On a single problem with one run each, this is exactly the place the method takes.

Which methods differ

Each pair of methods is tested: could \(P_{ij}\) be one half, so that neither does better more often? The test is a t-test over the problem sets, like the interval of an average. Since every pair is tested, the p-values are corrected for the number of pairs (Holm's method), so the chance that chance alone separates any pair stays below 5 %. Methods in a row of the standings that no test tells apart share a shaded band. That does not prove they are equal; with more problem sets, a difference might show. A method you add with a key is tested among its own pairs, so it never changes the verdict between the others.

Comparisons won

The chance that a method does better than another, averaged over the other methods, with a tie counting as half. It is the average place on a fixed scale, \(w_j = (m - \bar{r}_j) / (m - 1)\): 100 % means a method beats every other method on every problem, and 50 % means it wins as often as it loses. Unlike the average place, it stays on the same 0 to 100 % scale when the number of methods changes. Its value still depends on which methods are compared: when a weak method drops out, the others' shares fall.

Same budget or same time

The ranking uses the methods and problem sets you select. Methods are compared either at the same budget number, a rough comparison since their units differ, or at the same time: each method at its largest finished budget that takes at most the chosen number of seconds per problem on our timing workstation. A method that has not been timed yet, or whose smallest budget already takes longer, is left out, and so is one without results on the same problems as the others. A method whose largest budget stays under the time limit is drawn hollow: with more budget, it might move up.

Primary metric

One ranking metric was chosen before the results were read: \(\log_{10}\) FVU on the held-out points. Which time limit or budget to compare at depends on the use case (an answer within a second, or the best answer within minutes), so the page shows the ranking at every setting, and a ranking should be quoted together with its setting. Rankings by any other metric are for browsing, and a label next to the metric says which kind is on screen.
