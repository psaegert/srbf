Ranks

Ranks answer another question: how do all methods compare at once? On each problem, the methods are placed 1st, 2nd, and so on by the chosen metric. A method's standing is its average place over the \(K\) problems, \(\bar{r}_j = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k} r_{jk}\), where each of a problem's two runs counts as a problem of its own.

What a rank measures

Every problem hands out the same places, so easy and hard problems count the same, and a narrow win counts as much as a wide one. Ranks therefore measure how consistently a method is ahead, while the paired comparisons measure by how much. Methods with the same value share the average of the places they occupy. A method without a usable formula for a problem is placed last on it, on every metric.

Groups

Friedman's test first asks whether the average places differ more than they would by chance if all methods were equally good. Groups are drawn only if they do. For \(m\) methods and \(K\) problems, the critical difference (Nemenyi test, 5 % level)

$$ \mathrm{CD} = q_{0.05} \sqrt{\frac{m (m + 1)}{6 K}} $$

is the smallest gap between two average places that is unlikely to be chance, allowing for the fact that every pair of methods is compared; \(q_{0.05}\) is the 5 % critical value of the studentized range for \(m\) methods, divided by \(\sqrt{2}\). Here \(K\) counts each of a problem's two runs separately. Methods closer than this share a shaded band: the data cannot tell them apart. That does not prove they are equal; with more problems, a difference might show.

Comparisons won

The share of one-on-one comparisons a method wins, with a tie counting as half. It is the average place on a fixed scale, \(w_j = (m - \bar{r}_j) / (m - 1)\): 100 % means a method beats every other method on every problem, and 50 % means it wins as often as it loses. Unlike the average place, it keeps its meaning when the number of methods changes, so it can be followed across budgets even when methods drop in or out.

Same budget or same time

The ranking uses the methods and problem sets you select. Methods are compared either at the same budget number, a rough comparison since their units differ, or at the same time: each method at its largest finished budget that takes at most the chosen number of seconds per problem on our timing workstation. A method that has not been timed yet, or whose smallest budget already takes longer, is left out, and so is one without results on the same problems as the others. A method whose largest budget stays under the time limit is drawn hollow: with more budget, it might move up.

Primary metric

One ranking metric was chosen before the results were read: \(\log_{10}\) FVU on the held-out points. Which time limit or budget to compare at depends on the use case (an answer within a second, or the best answer within minutes), so the page shows the ranking at every setting, and a ranking should be quoted together with its setting. Rankings by any other metric are for browsing, and a label next to the metric says which kind is on screen.
