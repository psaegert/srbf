Benchmarks

srbf does not make up its own problems. It collects the benchmarks that papers on symbolic regression use and runs every method on all of them, the same way. This page lists them, says what kind of test each one is, and explains what srbf decided where a benchmark left something open, and which benchmarks it could not include.

The problem sets

What the tags mean

A problem set carries a tag when it holds for at least half of its problems. The tags describe the problems as srbf runs them.

Where the problems come from

science formula
Laws and models from physics and the other sciences.
classic benchmark
The test functions that papers on genetic programming and symbolic regression have used for decades.
generated formulas
Formulas drawn at random by a program, often by the generator a method was trained with.
measured data
Data from experiments or observations.
synthetic data
Data a program made: simulations, the outputs of other models, or data files the authors generated.

What a problem set tests

extrapolation
The test points lie outside the region the fitting points come from.
several tests
The authors test on more than one set of points; each test is a group of its own.
no test split
The authors name no test data, so a method is scored only on how well its formula fits the data it was given.
dummy inputs
Some inputs do not appear in the formula; a method has to find out which ones matter.
integer inputs
Some inputs take whole numbers only.
fixed points
The authors give the exact points to fit, and srbf uses them.
noisy data
The data carries noise, as published. (srbf sets the noise of formula problems itself.)
srbf-modified
The authors left part of the setup open, and srbf completed it. These problem sets carry a star (*) in their name.

Licenses

The license column names the license of what srbf took from a benchmark: its data, or the code or files that define its formulas. "formulas only" means srbf took only formulas, constants and input ranges, which are facts, from a paper or page without a license of its own.

How srbf runs a benchmark

Formulas

Most problem sets are formulas. srbf takes each formula, its constants, the ranges and distributions of its inputs and any inputs the formula does not use from the benchmark's authors. srbf itself chooses how many points a method sees (512 to fit, 512 to test) and the noise, the same for every formula problem; constants that the authors draw at random are drawn once per problem. Where the authors fix the exact points (for example x = 1, 2, ..., 50), srbf uses those points.

Data

Some problem sets are data: measurements, simulations or files the authors published, with or without a known formula. srbf uses the data as published. Each run fits at most 512 rows and tests on at most 512 rows, drawn afresh. srbf tests only where the authors name test data or a way to choose it; where they do not, a method is scored only on the data it fitted, and the problem set carries the tag "no test split".

Tests

Where the authors give test points, srbf tests on them. Where they give several (for example inside and beyond the training range), each is a group of its own. Where a formula benchmark gives none, srbf tests on fresh points from the training distribution.

Groups

A benchmark keeps the groups its authors divide it into (for example easy, medium and hard), however few problems a group holds. srbf sets a division aside only when it does not describe the problems themselves (a setting of the authors' generator, a variant of their protocol) or when two divisions overlap. A group with more than 1,024 problems is run on one fixed sample of 1,024, chosen by a hash of each problem's name, so it is the same on every machine.

What srbf decided

Where a benchmark left something open, or exists in copies that differ, srbf had to choose. These are the choices.

The first publication

Several classic problems circulate in later copies that differ from the paper that introduced them. srbf follows the first publication. Keijzer-6 is the harmonic number 1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/x (Keijzer 2003), the amplitude of Korns-7 is 213.80940889 (Korns 2011), Vladislavleva-7 is (x1 - 3)(x2 - 3) + 2 sin((x1 - 4)(x2 - 4)) (Vladislavleva et al. 2009), and Sine is sin(x) on x = 0, 0.1, ..., 6.2 (Poli et al., A Field Guide to Genetic Programming, 2008). Keijzer-5, Poly-10, and Nguyen-9 and -10 use the input ranges of their papers.

Fixed points

Some authors train on fixed points (a grid such as x = 1, 2, ..., 50) rather than on a distribution. srbf keeps these points and holds such problems as data, in a problem set named "... (fixed points)": Keijzer-1 to -4 and -6 to -9, Neat-6, Sine, Vladislavleva-2 and -3, Nonic, R1 to R3 and R1a to R3a, and the fixed-point problems of the newer benchmarks. They are tested on the authors' test points where the authors give them.

ERBench

ERBench builds three problems from every formula: the fitting points come from a window half as wide as the input range, placed at its start, middle or end, and the test points from the rest of the range. Each input is drawn from its own mixture of one to three parts, and a problem may get one input the formula does not use. srbf runs ERBench the same way, with its own point counts and noise. SynEq and OEIS are run on a fixed sample of 1,024 problems each.

FastSRB and SRSD

FastSRB's Feynman laws use the ranges of SRSD (Matsubara et al.). Where SRSD's own files disagree, srbf follows SRSD's code and published data: I.44.4's n lies in [1e24, 1e26], and whole-number inputs (quantum numbers, charges) are drawn as SRSD draws them. SRSD-Feynman with dummy inputs takes SRSD's own dummy inputs: the same number per problem, the same positions and the same distribution, read from SRSD's published data.

EQL robot arm*

EQL's authors state only that the joint angles of their robot arm follow sinusoids with an amplitude of up to pi/2 (pi for the extrapolation test). srbf draws each angle as (pi/2) sin(u), with u uniform on [0, 2 pi): the values such a sinusoid takes at random times. The extrapolation test uses the amplitude pi.

BMS Bessel functions*

The Bayesian machine scientist (BMS) fits Bessel functions of orders 0 to 4 in two studies and does not state all of their points. Without noise, it fits 10 points per order in [0, 2] and measures the error on [0, 4]; srbf tests on 100 points per order drawn uniformly on [0, 4], once and fixed. With noise, it uses 100 points per order in [0, 4]; srbf draws them uniformly.

RSRM's Gaussian curves

For RSRM's Gaussian distribution functions, srbf fits on the range RSRM fits on (x = 0 to 100) and tests on its extrapolation range (x = -100 to -1).

EvoMO-SR's second test

EvoMO-SR tests its four formulas twice: on the training distribution, and on points where exactly one input lies outside the training box (between 1.2 and 2 times its half-width, on either side) while the others stay inside. srbf keeps both tests, as groups "id" and "ood", and picks the moved input at random for each point.

Constant targets

On some ERBench problems every point of a run has the same target value (for example when an integer sequence is constant on a short window). srbf keeps these problems. On such a run the measures of fit quality are undefined and left out; the comparison of formulas works as usual.

Two checks dropped

Const-Test-1 and -2 are dropped from the Constant problem set: they are checks of a software package, not benchmark problems.

What srbf could not include

No input ranges

A formula is a problem only together with the inputs it is evaluated on. For 202 problems in 13 benchmarks the authors give no input ranges, and none could be found in their papers, supplements or code: PhysGym (77 problems; an agent chooses the inputs), ChatSR's knowledge set (50), the KAN paper's Feynman laws without units (20), Phy-SRBench (18), ERBench's older formula list (13) and AI Feynman 2.0's modular equations (11), both from the same table, EGRL-SR (3), symbolicregression.com's first test suite (3), FlowSR's synthetic laws (3), SurfaceBench's NCS10, DRSR's D_mixed, and Case 14 of Chen, Luo and Jiang in two copies.

Already included

Some collections repeat problems srbf already runs. The PMLB compilations of Friedman's functions (62), the Orzechowski et al. study (94) and the GECCO 2022 qualification track (20) are part of SRBench 2021's black-box problems, and nearly all of the PMLB regression collection (152) is in SRBench 2021 or in other problem sets srbf runs; E2E's 57 black-box problems are a selection of SRBench 2021's. SRBench 2021's Feynman data files and AI Descartes' noisy Feynman data sample the AI Feynman laws srbf already holds. SymbolicArena gathers problems from other benchmarks, which srbf runs from their own sources (apart from LLM-SRBench, below). 26 laws of the dimensionless AI Feynman version reduce to constants; srbf holds them with their units.

No license, or incomplete

LLM-SRBench (LSR-Transform and LSR-Synth) is published without a license, and some of its problems lack their constants. KAN's special functions give no input ranges and leave two of their outputs unspecified.

Too large, or not published

CAMELS (a 15.8 GB download), PhyE2E's lunar tide (13.3 million rows) and the Million Song Dataset (375 MB) are too large to run as a problem set. syren's data would have to be regenerated with a cosmology code, and a few benchmarks never published their data.

One very long formula

OccamNet's P7 is a piecewise function. Written with the operators srbf supports, it is 6,762 symbols long, more than the formula reader accepts (4,096).

One kind of test

SciLaws-Real defines two kinds of problems. srbf runs Type I; Type II tests a formula on held-out groups of data, which needs a protocol srbf does not have yet.
